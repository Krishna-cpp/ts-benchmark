Which forecast was right?

By Krishna. Part of tsbench, my forecasting benchmark.

I gave nine forecasting models the same job: look at a time series up to some date, then predict what comes next. Then I moved the date forward and asked again, ten or twelve times per dataset. The models never got to see the future, so every forecast below was a real guess.

Pick a dataset and step through the forecasts. The black line is what actually happened. The shaded band around each forecast is the range the model was 80% sure about.

Colour tells you what kind of model drew a line: gray for simple baselines, blue for classical statistics, orange for machine learning, and green for IBM's pretrained TTM model. Within a colour, solid, dashed and dotted lines tell the models apart. The faint shaded band around a line is that model's 80% range.

Over all the forecasts

In the middle column, the dot is the model's average error, coloured by model type as in the chart. The gray line through it is the 95% range for that average: if you reran the benchmark on similar dates, the average would most likely land inside it. The dotted vertical line marks 1, the error of just repeating the last value. Long, overlapping gray lines mean two models can't really be told apart.

How to read the numbers

MASE is the forecast error divided by the error you would get by predicting "same as last time" on the training data. That puts every dataset on the same scale. Lower is better, and the dotted line in the table marks 1.

A model marked worse lost to the best model by more than chance explains (paired Wilcoxon test across the forecast dates, Holm-corrected, 5% level). Everything else is tied with the best: with only ten or twelve dates, the data can't separate them.

If a model's band is honest, about 80% of the real values land inside it. Much lower means the model was overconfident.
